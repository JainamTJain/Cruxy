## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** fewer battlefield errors through machine speed *versus* keeping moral judgment and accountability for killing in human hands.

**The trap:** the case mixes two questions: (1) should lethal decisions be automated, and (2) who gets to set the limits, a company or the government? Answer them separately.

| Who | Stake | Voice in the case? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civilians in conflict zones | Being misidentified as targets; or being protected by better accuracy | No |
| Soldiers | Safety; moral injury; responsibility for outcomes | Barely |
| U.S. citizens | Freedom from mass surveillance | Indirect |
| AI companies and their workers | Complicity in uses they find wrong | Yes |
| Military and elected officials | Security; democratic authority over force | Yes |
| Adversaries and future conflicts | Escalation risk if war gets cheaper to wage | No |

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| If AI became as accurate as humans, would the ethical objection disappear? (the case's own question) | No: accuracy answers "will it hit the right target?" but not "who is responsible?" or "will war become too easy?" |
| Should private AI companies be able to control how governments use their tools in warfare and surveillance? (team's version) | A company may refuse to supply uses it judges wrong, but the binding limits belong in public law, not in either side's private say-so. |
| Is it permissible to use AI in lethal military decisions? | As decision support under meaningful human judgment, yes; as the decider, no. |
| Is AI just a neutral tool? | No: systems that rank and recommend targets embed human choices about data and values. |

**Our thesis:** Accuracy alone would not settle the question, because killing requires a responsible human judgment that can't be delegated; and while a company may refuse uses it believes are wrong, the lasting limits on lethal autonomy and domestic surveillance should be set by democratic law.

## 4. Claim 1: Even a more accurate AI would not remove the core objection: decisions to kill need meaningful human judgment and a responsible human.

**Logic line:** Because autonomous killing creates a responsibility gap (S1), humans "in the loop" drift into rubber-stamping (S2), and greater distance makes war easier to start (S3), accuracy alone cannot justify removing human judgment.

**S1. The responsibility gap.** When a machine selects and engages a target, no one may be fully answerable: not the programmer, the commander, or the machine.
*Backing (framework):* philosopher Robert Sparrow's "Killer Robots" (2007) argues that just war requires someone who can be held responsible for each death. Our team put it simply: letting AI use weapons turns a moral decision into a program.

**S2. Automation bias.** People formally "in the loop" come to defer to the system's recommendation.
*Backing (case fact):* the case warns that a human may remain in the loop but rely on the system, and that a well-timed suggestion can steer a decision. The wargame shows the models treated nuclear escalation as a manageable risk, which is exactly the kind of judgment we wouldn't want people deferring to.

**S3. Distance lowers the threshold for war.** Each step that removes risk from the side using force makes force easier to choose.
*Backing (example):* the team's progression: hands, knife, bow, gun, drone, AI. The case notes drone warfare already distances a country from its soldiers' danger, and that efficient, low-risk war may be easier to start and harder to restrain.

**Ethical analysis.** The values in tension are protecting lives through precision and protecting moral agency and accountability. Kant's humanity formula (never treat people merely as tools or data points) explains why a target is owed a human judgment. Consequences matter too: if war becomes cheaper, more of it may happen.
**We concede:** AI can reduce some errors in data analysis, so decision support under human judgment is acceptable.

## 5. Claim 2: A company may refuse uses it judges unethical, but the binding limits on lethal autonomy and domestic mass surveillance belong in public law.

**Logic line:** Because makers share responsibility for foreseeable uses of what they build (S1), democratic governments, not vendors, must set national policy (S2), and mass surveillance threatens the democratic accountability the military itself relies on (S3), the right answer is public, legal guardrails rather than private vetoes.

**S1. Makers can be complicit in foreseeable uses.** A company that knowingly supplies a tool for a use it believes is wrong shares responsibility for that use.
*Backing (example, outside the case):* in 2018 Google employees protested the Pentagon's Project Maven drone-imagery program, and Google declined to renew the contract. Conscientious refusal by builders has precedent.

**S2. Democratic legitimacy cuts the other way too.** Unelected companies shouldn't set military policy for a democracy, as the Pentagon argues in the case. That means the durable fix is law.
*Backing (outside the case):* U.S. Defense Department Directive 3000.09 already requires "appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force" for autonomous weapons. Our position strengthens and enforces public rules like this rather than relying on vendor contracts.

**S3. Mass surveillance of citizens undermines democracy itself.** People who know they are always watched speak and organize less freely.
*Backing (framework):* this is a chilling-effect argument rooted in privacy and free-association values. A limit on domestic mass surveillance protects the democratic accountability that gives the military its authority.

**Ethical analysis.** The values in tension are individual and corporate conscience versus democratic control of force. Virtue ethics asks what a responsible builder does; the answer includes refusing to sell for uses it believes are wrong. But a democracy can't let any one company decide national policy.
**We concede:** a government may choose another vendor. What we reject is either side making the rules alone.

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "AI is a neutral tool like Excel, refusing it costs lives, and only the law, not a company, should bind the military."**
*Their supports:* (1) war already involves human panic, bias and fatigue; (2) AI could identify threats faster and more accurately; (3) the military answers to elected officials, companies don't.
*Our response:* **Concede** (3): that's why Claim 2 puts the limits in law. **Distinguish** (2): we accept decision support; the objection is to delegating the kill decision. **Rebut** the neutrality premise: a system that ranks targets embeds choices about training data, thresholds and acceptable error. Excel doesn't choose who lives.

**Counterclaim: "If AI is proven more accurate, refusing it is itself unethical."**
*Our response:* "Proven" accuracy in tests isn't accuracy in the fog of war, where adversaries spoof data. And even perfect accuracy leaves the responsibility gap (S1) and the lower threshold for war (S3).

## 8. Judge question bank

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| If AI were provably more accurate and saved civilians, would you still require a human? | Yes, because accountability and the threshold for war don't depend on accuracy; but we'd welcome AI as decision support. |
| Isn't a human "in the loop" just a rubber stamp? | Often, which is why we require meaningful judgment: time to review, authority to refuse, and training against automation bias. |
| What about fast defensive systems that shoot down incoming missiles? | Different: they target munitions, not people, in seconds. Our concern is decisions to kill people. |
| Should a company be able to veto a democratic government? | Not veto: it may refuse to sell. The binding rules should be public law. |
| Flip test: would you defend a company refusing to sell to a government you like? | Yes, the same principle applies to any vendor and any government. |
| What would change your mind? | Evidence that meaningful human judgment can't be preserved at all with AI support, which would push us toward stricter limits, not looser ones. |

## 9. Commentary kit

- If they argue AI is neutral: ask how training data and thresholds can be value-free.
- If they argue companies should decide: ask what happens when a less careful company sells the same tool.
- If they argue only accuracy matters: ask who is responsible when an accurate system makes its rare mistake.
