# Case 9: GEM in the Rough (detailed review)

**One-line version: title to Cairo, partnership with London.**

## 1. The case at a glance

| Fact | Why it matters to us |
| --- | --- |
| The Grand Egyptian Museum (GEM) opened in Cairo on November 1, 2025, holding 50,000+ artifacts spanning 5,000 years | Removes the "Egypt can't care for it" objection |
| The French found the Rosetta Stone in the 1790s; the British took it shortly after; it has been in London since | Acquired through war, not purchase or gift |
| The British Museum says Egypt's government has made no formal request, and the British Museum Act 1963 "places strict limitations on returning objects" | The museum's two shields: procedure and law |
| Greece asked for the Parthenon Marbles in the 1980s; the Act helped block it; talks continue, with Greece offering loans to "fill the void" | A model for a deal that leaves London something |
| Uganda and Ghana received objects from England on loan in 2024, without ownership | Loans without title are today's compromise |
| UCLA's Fowler Museum voluntarily returned seven artifacts to Ghana in 2024, citing an ethical duty for objects taken violently or coercively | Institutions can act first |
| NAGPRA (1990, recently updated) makes museums, not tribes, start repatriation, with consent needed before display | The holder carries the burden of acting |
| Deaccessioning costs staff time and money and, some say, erodes "public trust" | The museum's strongest practical points |

**Outside the case (verify before use):** the Stone passed to Britain under Article 16 of the 1801 Capitulation of Alexandria, and the British Museum's defense is that an Ottoman admiral signed it; Zahi Hawass's 2022 petition passed 100,000 signatures; the Stone is one of 100,000+ Egyptian and Sudanese objects in the British Museum; the Charities Act 2022 moral-obligation power took effect in November 2025 but excluded 16 national museums, including the British Museum; the Human Tissue Act 2004 (s. 47) and a 2009 Holocaust act already allow some returns; in 2023 the British Museum disclosed about 2,000 objects missing or stolen.

**An opening image.** The Stone is a 196 BC decree honoring Ptolemy V, a king of Macedonian descent, carved in three scripts so one decision could be read by every community it touched: a foreign-rooted power earning legitimacy by deferring to Egyptian institutions. That is the move we ask of the British Museum.

## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** righting a wartime taking *versus* preservation, global access and the law.

**The trap:** cultural nationalism ("objects belong to their nation") invites a slippery slope that empties every museum. Our case rests on a narrow four-part test instead.

| Who | Stake | Voice in the case? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Egyptian public | Access to and ownership of its heritage | Through officials and petitions |
| The British Museum and its visitors | Collection, prestige, free access | Yes |
| Egyptian scholars and conservators | Who gets to become an expert on their own past | No |
| Other claimant nations (Greece, Nigeria, Ghana) | Precedent | Indirect |
| Future generations | Preservation | No |

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| Is it morally permissible for museums to retain artifacts that were previously stolen? (Dr. B's version) | Not once a people with a continuous tie asks for it and can care for it; "stolen" covers a range, so terms of return can vary, but the holder's convenience never settles the question. |
| Should the British Museum return the Rosetta Stone? | Yes: by transferring ownership to Egypt, with mutual public-access commitments and a reciprocal partnership. |

**Our four-part test.** Return is owed when all four hold: (1) **coercive acquisition**, taken by force, conquest or duress; (2) **a continuous claimant** with an unbroken tie to the object's place and culture; (3) **central significance** to that people's identity; (4) **capacity to care and share**, including keeping it publicly accessible. The Stone meets all four; most of a collection fails at least one.

**Our thesis:** A museum may not keep a coercively taken object once a people with a continuous tie asks for it and can care for it; the Rosetta Stone should go to Egypt with a reciprocal partnership, negotiated now.

## 4. Claim 1: The British Museum's title is defective, and the duty to repair falls on the institution that still benefits.

**Logic line:** Because the Stone changed hands as a spoil of war between empires (S1), keeping taken objects for being "the better caretaker" can't be a universal rule (S2), and responsibility for an inherited wrong follows power and benefit (S3), the museum carries the duty to return it.

**S1. A broken first link.** A holding is just only if it was justly acquired and justly transferred.
*Backing (framework and outside fact):* Robert Nozick's entitlement theory (1974), a pro-property theory, condemns this title: a handover from the army that took it to the army that took it from them. An Ottoman admiral's signature isn't Egyptian consent.

**S2. The caretaker maxim fails universalizability.**
*Backing (framework, Dr. B):* Kant's test: if everyone kept what they took whenever they judged themselves better caretakers, ownership would belong to the strongest and best-funded, destroying the property claim the museum relies on. And "we know better how to care for your heritage" is paternalism that treats Egyptians as less than equal moral agents.

**S3. Responsibility, not blame.** Today's trustees took nothing, but they hold the power and the benefit.
*Backing (framework):* Iris Marion Young's parameters (power, privilege, interest, collective ability) put a heavy duty on a wealthy national institution. W.D. Ross lists reparation among our basic duties.

**Ethical analysis.** The values are justice for a historic wrong versus stewardship. Corrective justice says whoever benefits from a wrong must repair it.
**We concede:** today's trustees bear no blame, and the British Museum has cared for the Stone well.

## 5. Claim 2: The classic reasons for keeping the Stone no longer hold, and return should come with a reciprocal partnership now.

**Logic line:** Because the GEM ties London on care and wins on connection (S1), the law already bends when the moral case is accepted (S2), and an early, structured return protects the public and serves both sides (S3), the Stone should go now with a partnership.

**S1. Cosmopolitanism, taken seriously, points to Cairo.**
*Backing (framework and case fact):* the universal-museum defenders (Appiah, Cuno) rest their case on care and access. With the GEM open, care is a tie, and a tie goes to where the object was made and is part of living identity. The 2002 "universal museum" declaration was signed by the directors of the museums holding contested objects: no one should judge their own case.

**S2. The law is a choice, not a wall.**
*Backing (outside facts):* the British Museum can already return human remains (Human Tissue Act 2004, s. 47) and Nazi-looted works (2009). Teammate's consistency challenge: if dignity justifies an exception for remains and justice one for Nazi loot, what principle excludes a spoil of colonial war?

**S3. Return well, and early.**
*Backing (examples, outside the case):* the Benin lesson: after Germany returned bronzes to Nigeria in 2022, a 2023 decree vested ownership in the Oba, raising public-access fears, so public display should be written into the transfer. In 2009 Egypt suspended cooperation with the Louvre until it returned five fresco fragments, which France did within weeks. An early, voluntary return buys loans and goodwill; a forced one buys nothing.

**Ethical analysis.** Make conditions mutual (both museums accept public-access and lending commitments) so they aren't neo-colonial. Return also redistributes expertise: Egyptian conservators shouldn't have to travel abroad to study their own heritage (capabilities approach).
**We concede:** the Stone may be seen by fewer people in raw numbers in Cairo; we answer with digital access and affordable entry as terms of the deal.

**What it looks like in practice (hold for Q&A):** open talks now rather than waiting for a formal request; transfer ownership with mutual commitments; London receives rotating loans, joint scholarship, a replica and an honest exhibit about how the Stone came to Britain; amend the 1963 Act or extend the Charities Act power.

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "Historic injustice fades; after 225 years the claim is superseded" (Jeremy Waldron, 1992).**
*Their supports:* (1) circumstances change, (2) enforcing old claims can create new injustice, (3) no one alive did wrong.
*Our response:* **Concede** Waldron is right about land, where return would displace people (the team's California example). **Distinguish:** returning one object out of 100,000+ displaces no one. **Rebut** (3): our claim rests on responsibility, not blame.

**Counterclaim: "Modern Egypt isn't the Stone's origin; it's Ptolemaic and one-third Greek" (raised by Daniel).**
*Our response:* it was made in Egypt, by Egypt's government at the time, for Egyptian temples, in two Egyptian scripts. Cultural identity isn't ethnic purity.

**Counterclaim: "If the Stone goes, so does half the museum."**
*Our response:* our four-part test applies object by object; fair purchases, gifts and objects with no continuous claimant stay.

## 8. Judge question bank

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| Should the Parthenon Marbles go back too? | Apply the test aloud; likely yes, and consistency is the point. |
| What if Egypt never formally asks? | The museum should make a standing public offer, but not force return on a state that declines. |
| Would you return objects fairly bought in 1900? | Generally no: a fair purchase fails the first test. |
| Isn't a replica in Cairo enough? | Flip it: if a replica is as good, London should keep the replica. |
| Egypt's pharaohs took objects from Nubia. Does Egypt owe Sudan? | Possibly, under the same test; a principle that only runs against Europe isn't a principle. |
| Rival claimants, like the Koh-i-Noor? | Rival claims among the wronged don't strengthen the taker's claim; negotiate shared custody. The Stone has one claimant. |
| Egypt's government will use it for prestige. | Probably; Britain has for 225 years. Heritage belongs to a people across governments, and public access is a condition. |
