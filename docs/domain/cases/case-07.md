# Case 7: Equal Access Activism (detailed review)

**One-line version: sue to fix, not to collect.**

## 1. The case at a glance

| Fact | Why it matters to us |
| --- | --- |
| The ADA passed in 1990 and is now 36 years old; it aims at equal opportunity, full participation and independent living | Owners have had a long time to learn the principle |
| The federal ADA allows no money damages; California's Unruh Act (Cal. Civ. Code § 51) allows $4,000 per denial of access | The money, and the incentive structure, comes from state law |
| Anthony Bouyer, paralyzed after spinal tumor surgery, carries a 12-inch level and sued three businesses on one block in September 2025; the *LA Times* (April 2026) reports one man filed 1,800 suits | Real harm and real standing, but volume raises the incentive question |
| Manning Law filed 9,000+ suits in Southern California over about a decade | Litigation as a business model |
| Owners cite zoning, historic buildings, website skills and shifting DOJ guidance; consultants don't always fix it | The knowledge problem is real, not an excuse |
| Lola's Chicken Shack owner Mark Rogers postponed brain surgery; a Richmond burger joint closed after 38 years when its landlord refused to pay for a ramp | Suits can produce closure instead of access; control sits with the landlord |
| Drive-through-only ordering shuts out deaf customers; the case calls such measures ones that "isolate and segregate" | The wrong is real |
| Disability Rights Advocates' 2025 wins: Chicago pedestrian signals, Medi-Cal notices in Braille, interpreters for deaf prisoners in Tennessee | High-impact suits against capable institutions fix barriers at scale |
| Bad Daddy's Burger Bar paid a $352,000 settlement after refusing to seat a youth wheelchair basketball team | Willful exclusion deserves a heavy penalty |

**Outside the case (verify before use):** California already cuts minimum damages to $2,000 for small businesses that fix a violation within 30 days (Cal. Civ. Code § 55.54); "high-frequency litigants" pay an extra $1,000 filing fee (AB 1615); AB 780 (2025) proposed no damages for violations fixed within 120 days of notice (check whether it passed); federal tax help exists (a Disabled Access Credit up to $5,000 a year and a $15,000 barrier-removal deduction); Title III requires barrier removal in existing buildings only where "readily achievable."

## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** equal access as a civil right *versus* fairness to owners who lack knowledge, money or control over their buildings.

**The trap:** attacking Bouyer's character. The case's real question is the design of the incentive: what the system pays for. Also never frame disabled people as a "burden" businesses are forced to accommodate.

| Who | Stake | Voice in the case? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Disabled customers | Equal standing in public life | Through Bouyer and DRA |
| Small business owners | Survival, fairness | Yes |
| Landlords | Control the structure | Barely (Richmond) |
| Serial-litigation firms | Fees | Yes |
| The next tenant | Inherits the same barrier after a closure | No |
| Disabled residents of the neighborhood | Lose local businesses when they close | No |

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question (from practice) | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| Is it acceptable to aim disability activism at small businesses as often as large ones? | Yes, the duty is the same; but penalties that ignore capacity are not acceptable. |
| Is it acceptable to sue and shut down businesses that can't afford, or aren't permitted, to comply? | No: a closure that leaves the barrier for the next tenant fails activism's own goal; aim the pressure at whoever controls the building. |
| Is Bouyer's serial litigation ethical? | As friction aimed at changing the built environment, yes; where it pays out without producing access, the fault lies in the system around him. |

**Our thesis:** The duty to be accessible is the same for every business, but enforcement is justified only when it brings barriers down, so it should scale with knowledge, control and capacity.

## 4. Claim 1: Accessibility lawsuits, including serial ones, are justified civil-rights enforcement.

**Logic line:** Because inaccessibility is a real wrong of exclusion (S1), the law deliberately recruited private enforcers (S2), and disability rights has always advanced through friction (S3), suing businesses that keep barriers is justified.

**S1. The wrong is real.** A ramp too steep to climb or a drive-through that sends deaf customers inside tells people the space is not for them.
*Backing (framework):* the social model of disability (Mike Oliver): the barrier, not the body, does the excluding. Elizabeth Anderson's democratic equality (1999): equality means standing as an equal in civil society, and shops are where that standing is lived.

**S2. Private enforcement is the system's design.** Title III has no damages and the Justice Department can't inspect every storefront; California added $4,000 precisely to recruit private enforcers.
*Backing (example):* bounties are a normal tool: the federal False Claims Act pays whistleblowers, and California's Prop 65 pays private enforcers. Condemning serial litigants while leaving public enforcement unfunded is incoherent.

**S3. Activism as friction.** Friction slows things down, but it also gives grip.
*Backing (framework and history):* Hamraie and Fritsch, "Crip Technoscience Manifesto" (2019, shared by Dr. B). In March 1990 activists crawled up the U.S. Capitol steps, and Berkeley activists earlier chipped away curbs to make their own curb cuts. Read Bouyer charitably, as part of this tradition.

**Ethical analysis.** The values in tension are equal standing and fairness to owners. Care ethics adds the two-step model from *NFB v. Scribd* (DRA's suit, which ended with Scribd making its library accessible): offer help first, then litigate if refused.
**We concede:** serial suits do real good, and willful exclusion (Bad Daddy's) deserves full penalties.

## 5. Claim 2: The per-visit bounty pays for findings, not fixes, so enforcement should be redesigned around repair and scaled to knowledge, control and capacity.

**Logic line:** Because a bounty rewards the violations cheapest to prove against the defendants least able to fight (S1), it can produce closures instead of ramps (S2), and responsibility for an inherited environment is shared and follows control (S3), the remedy should pay for repair and fall hardest on those with power.

**S1. Selection effect.** Litigation economics favors small defendants who settle and violations provable with a 12-inch level.
*Backing (framework):* Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Steven Shavell (1984): after-the-fact lawsuits work badly when defendants are "judgment-proof" and the regulator knows the standards better than they do. Small, old businesses fail every test; chains and public agencies pass them.

**S2. Compliance by subtraction.** The cheapest legal "fix" can be removing something rather than adding access.
*Backing (case fact and framework):* Lola's was sued over outdoor tables; removing them helps no wheelchair user. The Richmond closure produced zero ramps. Derek Parfit's "leveling down" objection: making some worse off without making anyone better off is no gain in equality.

**S3. Responsibility follows control and capacity.**
*Backing (framework and team idea):* Iris Marion Young's social connection model: responsibility for structural injustice grows with power, privilege and ability to act. The Richmond landlord controlled the structure. Teammate framing: "same charge, different penalty," as in sentencing. StopGap makes cheap ramps, so many fixes are affordable.

**Ethical analysis.** Judge the remedy by what it repairs, not what it transfers. Curb cuts serve everyone (the curb-cut effect), so the cost of an old, inaccessible world shouldn't fall on the smallest shoulders.
**We concede:** our claim that bounty enforcement tilts the market toward chains is a hypothesis; the case gives two data points, not a study.

**What it looks like in practice (hold for Q&A):** two tracks (full damages for public bodies, chains and willful exclusion; inspection and a certified fix for small businesses); pay enforcers for completed fixes; no credit for subtraction; a public retrofit fund plus landlord co-responsibility; posted accessibility grades like restaurant health grades (Bethany's idea); and fund the replacement enforcement *before* narrowing damages.

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "Thirty-six years is notice enough. If you can't serve everyone, you shouldn't run a public business" (Kiyota's opening).**
*Their supports:* (1) no one can plead ignorance of the Civil Rights Act; (2) every day of delay excludes someone; (3) without the $4,000, nobody sues.
*Our response:* **Concede** (2) and (3) matter, so full damages stay for willful and large defendants, and enforcers are paid for fixes. **Distinguish** (1): not discriminating by race costs nothing, you simply stop; removing a physical barrier takes capital, which is why the ADA limits the duty in existing buildings to what is "readily achievable." **Rebut** the outcome logic: the Richmond suit produced a closed restaurant and no ramp, forever.

**Counterclaim: "Your proposal is just H.R. 620, which every disability group fought."**
*Our response:* **Distinguish:** H.R. 620 (passed by the House in 2018) added a waiting period and nothing else, cutting enforcement without replacing it. We change what enforcement pays for and fund public inspection first.

**Counterclaim: "Why should my taxes pay for barriers I didn't create?"**
*Our response (Kiyota's line):* taxes already fund schools, transit and courts not everyone uses; curb cuts benefit everyone; and anyone can become disabled.

## 8. Judge question bank

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| Bouyer files suit after suit and earns money. Does that change your evaluation? | No: we judge the act by whether it removes barriers. His level shows how common barriers still are. |
| Is drive-through-only ordering discrimination? | Yes, without an equal alternative; text or screen ordering is cheap, so it's readily achievable. |
| Who was at fault in Richmond? | Mainly the landlord, who controlled the structure. |
| Mark Rogers postponed brain surgery. Does hardship matter morally? | It matters to the remedy, not the duty. |
| How would you know whether lawsuits are working? | The share of suits that end in a completed fix. The case doesn't give it; that's the empirical crux. |
| Isn't your proposal a gift to businesses? | No: large and willful defendants face the same or harsher penalties, and small ones still must fix the barrier. |
| Change the facts: what if Bouyer gave his settlements to a ramp fund? | Better, but the selection and subtraction problems remain; the fix is structural. |

## 9. Commentary kit

- If they defend the lawsuits fully: ask whether every suit should end in a fix, and what the Richmond closure achieved.
- If they condemn the lawsuits as extortion: ask who enforces the ADA otherwise, and how they answer Bad Daddy's and DRA's results.
- If they attack Bouyer personally: motive doesn't change whether a barrier is real.
