# Case 3: Sister City No More (detailed review)

## 1. The case at a glance

| Fact | Source in the case |
| --- | --- |
| In 2018 Osaka cut its sister-city ties with San Francisco over a statue honoring "comfort women" | BBC News, April 4, 2018 |
| "Comfort women" were women trafficked and sexually abused by the Japanese Imperial Army during World War II | Case text |
| The statue, "Women's Column of Strength," stands near San Francisco's Chinatown and honors thousands of Korean and Chinese women and all women affected by such crimes | World Heritage USA |
| The memorial's stated values: memory, dialogue, peace and reconciliation, transformation and justice | World Heritage USA |
| Japan's embassy objected to a similar planned statue in New Zealand, warning of diplomatic harm; the council rejected it, citing lack of public support | BBC and Reuters, April 2026 |
| New Zealand opponents cited community tension, political readings and relevance; supporters cited reflection, acknowledging harm and education | Case text |
| Japan has offered few apologies or reparations that survivors consider satisfactory | Comfort Women Justice Coalition |
| San Francisco has far larger Chinese and Korean communities than New Zealand, and the U.S. military reportedly took part in the abuse; New Zealand had no such involvement | NBC News, April 27, 2007 |
| Both countries should examine monuments to their own wrongs: many U.S. statues honor conquistadors and Confederates, few honor Native Americans or enslaved people, and plantations operate as wedding venues | Case text |
| Framing question: how should governments decide which events to memorialize, weighing culpability, resources and national memory? | Case text |

**Outside facts (verify before use):** Japan's government issued the Kono Statement acknowledging the military's involvement in 1993, and Japan and South Korea reached a 2015 agreement including a roughly one-billion-yen fund, which many survivors rejected.

## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** the right of victims and communities to public memory *versus* diplomatic harmony and a nation's interest in how its past is portrayed.

**The trap:** the case looks like it's about Japan, but its last third turns the question on the U.S. A team that condemns Japan while ignoring Confederate statues and plantation weddings will be caught in a double standard. Our claims are built to apply to both.

| Who | Stake | Voice in the case? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surviving comfort women and their families | Recognition, dignity, truth | Barely |
| Korean and Chinese American communities in San Francisco | Public acknowledgment of their history | Indirect |
| Japanese and Japanese American communities | Fair portrayal; not being blamed collectively | No |
| City governments | Choosing what public space honors | Yes |
| National governments | Diplomacy, national image | Yes |
| Descendants of the enslaved and Native Americans | Whether U.S. memory is honest about U.S. wrongs | Raised at the end of the case |

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| How should governments decide which events to memorialize in public spaces? (the case's question) | By four tests: a real connection to the place or its people, the voices of victims at the center, a purpose of remembrance and education rather than shaming, and the same standard applied to our own history. |
| Was San Francisco right to keep the statue despite losing its sister city? | Yes: it meets all four tests, and diplomatic pressure is not a moral reason to erase memory. |
| Was New Zealand wrong to reject its statue? | Not necessarily: its connection was weaker and its community didn't support it, so the decision is defensible under the same tests. |
| Should the U.S. change its own monuments? | Yes: the same tests require honest memory of slavery and Indigenous dispossession. |

**Our thesis:** Public memorials are justified when a community has a real connection to the history, victims' voices are at the center, and the purpose is remembrance rather than shaming a present-day people; by that standard San Francisco was right to keep its memorial, and the U.S. owes the same honesty about its own wrongs.

## 4. Claim 1: San Francisco was justified in keeping the memorial, and diplomatic pressure is not a valid reason to remove it.

**Logic line:** Because San Francisco has a real connection to this history (S1), memorials serve justice for victims whose suffering hasn't been adequately acknowledged (S2), and the statue honors victims rather than accusing today's Japanese people (S3), keeping it was justified.

**S1. Real connection.** San Francisco has large Chinese and Korean communities, and the U.S. military reportedly took part in the abuse, so the U.S. shares some accountability.
*Backing (case fact):* the case contrasts San Francisco with New Zealand on both points (NBC News, 2007).

**S2. Memory is a form of justice owed to victims.** When wrongs go unacknowledged, public memory is one of the few remedies left.
*Backing (framework):* corrective justice says whoever is wronged is owed repair; philosopher Avishai Margalit's *The Ethics of Memory* (2002) argues that communities have duties to remember grave wrongs. The case reports that survivors consider Japan's apologies and reparations unsatisfactory.

**S3. Remembrance is not accusation.** The statue honors "all other women affected by such crimes," not only victims of Japan.
*Backing (team idea):* in practice we put it this way: the goal is "not to shame a country, but to show what people are capable of."

**Ethical analysis.** The values in tension are victims' dignity and truth versus diplomatic harmony and national face (the team noted that saving face is culturally significant in Japan). Kant's humanity formula supports the victims: their suffering can't be treated as a bargaining chip in diplomatic relations.
**We concede:** sister-city ties have real value, and losing them was a cost.

## 5. Claim 2: The right standard for public memory has four tests, and it binds the U.S. as much as Japan.

**Logic line:** Because a principle we apply to others must apply to ourselves (S1), societies that confront their own wrongs show it can be done well (S2), and the community most connected to a history should decide, with victims at the center (S3), the U.S. must hold its own monuments to the same four tests.

**S1. Consistency.** Memorializing another country's wrongs while honoring our own perpetrators is a double standard.
*Backing (framework and case fact):* Kant's universalizability test: act only on a rule everyone could follow. The case notes many U.S. statues honor conquistadors and Confederate leaders while few honor Native Americans or enslaved people.

**S2. Acknowledgment works.** Countries can face their own atrocities without collapse.
*Backing (example):* Germany's public memory of the Holocaust, including Berlin's Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe and preserved camps used as public history, which the case contrasts with U.S. plantations used as wedding venues. Our team flagged Germany as the model: acknowledgment, remediation, and no denial.

**S3. Local communities decide, with victims at the center.**
*Backing (case fact and framework):* New Zealand's council rejected its statue for lack of local support, while San Francisco's communities wanted theirs; the same test gives different answers. Serene Khader's *Decolonizing Universalism* (in the team's resources) argues we can hold universal values against injustice without imposing one culture's way of expressing them.

**Ethical analysis.** This claim protects us from moral relativism (anything goes if it's another culture) and from moral imperialism (only other countries need to change).
**We concede:** some U.S. monuments can be kept with added context, such as plaques, rather than removed; the test is what the public space honors.

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "Memorials abroad inflame nationalism, damage today's relations, and ignore that Japan has apologized."**
*Their supports:* (1) Japan's government acknowledged the military's role (Kono Statement, 1993) and reached a 2015 agreement with South Korea; (2) present-day Japanese people aren't responsible; (3) a city shouldn't conduct foreign policy.
*Our response:* **Concede** (2): that's why S3 of Claim 1 matters. **Distinguish** (3): honoring victims in your own city isn't foreign policy. **Rebut** (1): apologies that survivors reject don't close the question, and using diplomatic ties to pressure a city to remove a memorial treats memory as a bargaining chip.

**Counterclaim: "Who are Americans to judge another culture's way of handling its past?"**
*Our response:* We apply the same test to ourselves (Claim 2), which is the difference between universal principle and cultural imperialism.
