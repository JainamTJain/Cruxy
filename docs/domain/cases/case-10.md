## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** a person's chance to move on from their past *versus* the public's right to accurate information and a free press.

**The trap:** treating all information alike. A court acquittal, a revenge-porn image, and a politician's own old posts raise different questions. Tier them.

| Who | Stake | Voice in the case? |

| Employers and landlords | Information for decisions | Yes |
| Journalists and historians | An accurate record | Indirect |
| Voters | Knowing who candidates are | Through the Platner example |
| People wrongly convicted | Clearing their names | No |

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| Do people have a right to be forgotten online? | A limited one: a right to have some information made harder to find, not a right to erase the record. |
| Was the Delhi court right to de-index acquittals? | Yes: de-indexing protects people the courts cleared while leaving the record intact for anyone with a real need. |
| Should public figures be able to erase their past posts? | No: people seeking public power get less protection for their own past public statements. |

**Our thesis:** People have a limited right to be forgotten: information about them that harms them without serving a legitimate public purpose (acquittals, old minor offenses, content posted without consent) may be made harder to find, but official records stay, and people seeking public power get less protection for what they themselves said.

## 4. Claim 1: De-indexing records of acquittals and minor, old offenses is permissible, and often required.

**Logic line:** Because an acquittal means the state failed to prove guilt (S1), permanent searchability punishes people beyond any sentence (S2), and de-indexing keeps the record while restoring "practical obscurity" (S3), the Delhi court's approach is justified.

**S1. Acquittal deserves respect.** An acquitted person is legally innocent; a search result that suggests otherwise contradicts the court.
*Backing (case fact):* the Delhi High Court's order applies to people acquitted or discharged.

**S2. Endless punishment is unjust.**
*Backing (framework):* Mill's harm principle cuts both ways: the information harms the person while serving no one's safety. Restorative justice (the team noted South Africa's truth-and-reconciliation model) aims at return to the community, not permanent exclusion.

**S3. Obscurity, not erasure.**
*Backing (outside the case):* the U.S. Supreme Court's idea of "practical obscurity" (1989): records once kept in courthouse files were public but hard to find. De-indexing restores that balance; anyone with a real need can still look.

**Ethical analysis.** The values in tension are a fresh start versus an accurate public record. Kant: treating someone as permanently defined by an accusation denies their capacity to change.
**We concede:** some employers (schools, hospitals, security roles) have legitimate needs to check records; our team's Finland-style model allows targeted checks for those roles (verify the details).

## 5. Claim 2: The right depends on the kind of information: consent and public power decide the tier.

**Logic line:** Because information posted without consent was never fairly public (S1), people seeking public power invite scrutiny of their own words (S2), and government and press records serve accountability (S3), the right must be tiered.

**S1. Consent: what was never fairly public should come down.**
*Backing (team idea and case fact):* our consent principle: information about a private person, posted by someone else without consent, may be removed (revenge porn is the clearest case). Add an age line: content from before 18 deserves extra protection.

**S2. Public power reduces protection.**
*Backing (case fact):* Platner deleted his posts, but voters have a legitimate interest in what a candidate said publicly. Officials get less leeway, and powerful people could otherwise use the right to scrub their records.

**S3. Records and journalism stay.**
*Backing (framework):* accountability requires a reliable record; Jeremy Waldron's work on dignity explains why the harm of exposure must be weighed against the public's need, not ignored.

**Ethical analysis.** This tiering avoids two failures: a right so broad it rewrites history, and a right so narrow it ignores victims of non-consensual exposure.
**We concede:** the line between private and public figures is blurry (a local influencer, a school board member).

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "Truthful information should never be suppressed; the public decides what matters."**
*Their supports:* (1) free press, (2) employers' right to know, (3) history must not be rewritten.
*Our response:* **Concede** (3): we never delete court records or journalism. **Distinguish:** de-indexing changes findability, not truth. **Rebut** (2): an acquittal isn't information about guilt; using it in hiring treats an innocent person as guilty.

**Counterclaim: "Powerful people will abuse this to erase scandals."**
*Our response:* **Concede** the risk; that's why S2 of Claim 2 gives public figures less protection and requires independent review.
