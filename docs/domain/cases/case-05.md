## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** accurate public forecasting and individual freedom to trade *versus* turning war, death and political violence into profit opportunities that reward inside knowledge.

**The trap:** "markets are accurate" is true and irrelevant to whether some markets are wrong. Also, don't argue for a universal ban, or a judge will ask about weather or Oscars markets. Draw the line by kind of event.

| Who | Stake | Voice in the case? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People in the countries being bet on | Their lives become wagers; markets may create incentives around their fate | No |
| Ordinary traders, many young | Losses, gambling harm, unequal information | Indirect |
| Insiders (soldiers, officials) | Temptation to profit from secret knowledge | Shown by the indictment |
| Platforms | Fees and growth | Yes (CEO quotes) |
| Public and policymakers | Better forecasts, or corrupted decisions | Indirect |

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| Are there geopolitical issues that allow trading without moral harm? (closing question) | Few: markets on war, death and the removal of leaders should not exist, while markets on scheduled public processes (elections, votes, economic data) can be permissible with safeguards. |
| Is betting on an election different from betting on a head of state's removal? | Yes: an election is a public, scheduled process no one can secretly decide; a removal often turns on violence and secret plans that insiders control. |
| Are prediction markets morally permissible? | Conditionally: it depends on what's being bet on, who can know the outcome early, and whether betting creates incentives to cause harm. |

**Our thesis:** Prediction markets are not wrong in themselves, but markets on war, death and secret political action are, because they reward insiders and turn human suffering into a commodity; markets on public, scheduled events can be permissible with strong safeguards.

## 4. Claim 1: Markets on war, death and the removal of leaders are morally wrong.

**Logic line:** Because these markets reward secret knowledge that insiders control (S1), they put a price on human lives and suffering (S2), and they can create incentives to cause or time the events themselves (S3), they should not exist.

**S1. Inside knowledge decides these outcomes.** When the event is a secret operation, the only people who can "forecast" well are the people planning it.
*Backing (case fact):* the soldier indicted for about $400,000 in trades on the Maduro operation, and the 150 accounts that bought the day before intervention in Iran.

**S2. Lives become commodities.** Betting on a person's death or a war treats the people affected as mere instruments for profit.
*Backing (framework):* Kant's humanity formula; Michael Sandel's *What Money Can't Buy* (2012) argues that putting a price on some goods corrupts them, using "death pools" on celebrities as an example. Our team's notes: markets strip context and treat people as means.

**S3. Incentives can bend the event.** When someone profits from an outcome they can influence, they have a reason to make it happen or time it.
*Backing (example, outside the case):* the U.S. government canceled DARPA's 2003 market on terrorism and coups after critics warned that people could profit from causing attacks.

**Ethical analysis.** The values in tension are freedom to trade and accurate information versus human dignity and fair play. Consequences point the same way: markets that leak secret operations can endanger soldiers and civilians.
**We concede:** these markets may produce accurate signals. Accuracy built on insiders profiting from war is not worth its cost.

## 5. Claim 2: Markets on public, scheduled events can be permissible, with safeguards for fairness and gambling harm.

**Logic line:** Because elections and economic data are public processes no single insider controls (S1), adults may risk their own money on their own judgment (S2), and strong safeguards can address insider trading and addiction (S3), these markets can be permissible.

**S1. Public processes resist insiders.** No one can secretly decide an election's outcome the way a commander decides an operation.
*Backing (case fact):* Kalshi's 2024 election markets forecast well, showing the information came from public signals.

**S2. Autonomy.** Adults may make risky choices with their own money.
*Backing (framework):* Mill's harm principle: limit freedom only to prevent harm to others. A bet on an election mainly risks the bettor's own money.

**S3. Safeguards work where gambling regulation already works.**
*Backing (team idea and example):* our notes compare this to warning labels on cigarettes and alcohol: allowed, but with friction, limits and clear information. Platforms can ban insiders from trading and add deposit limits.

**Ethical analysis.** Soft paternalism lets us protect people from uninformed or compulsive choices without banning informed ones. Our team observed that these apps are heavily advertised and have no "house" limiting losses; that's an argument for friction, not prohibition.
**We concede:** election markets could themselves affect voter behavior or be manipulated; watch for this.

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "Prediction markets are the most accurate forecasting tool, and accurate information saves lives, even about war."**
*Their supports:* (1) markets aggregate dispersed knowledge, (2) Kalshi's 2024 track record, (3) governments and businesses can plan better.
*Our response:* **Concede** markets can forecast well. **Distinguish:** in war markets, the "information" is often leaked secrets, which is the harm. **Rebut** (3): the same accuracy is possible without money on death; forecasting tournaments like the Good Judgment Project do it without betting on lives (verify before citing).

**Counterclaim: "It's just like the stock market; you're betting on defense stocks when war comes."**
*Our response:* **Distinguish:** stocks fund companies and have a productive purpose; a contract on whether a leader is killed exists only to bet on the event. Insider trading is also illegal in stocks for the same reason we object here.

## 8. Judge question bank

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| What about a market on whether a war ends? | Still betting on war, and insiders still hold the key information; we'd exclude it. |
| Is a weather market permissible? | Yes: no one controls the weather and no one is commodified. |
| If insider trading were perfectly enforced, would war markets be okay? | No: Claim 1's S2 and S3 still hold. |
| Isn't your line arbitrary? | Our line has three tests: who controls the outcome, whether lives are the subject, and whether betting creates an incentive to cause harm. |
| Should the government ban them, or just you not bet? | Platforms should not list them; individuals shouldn't trade them; law can follow. |
| Change the facts: what if profits went to humanitarian aid? | Better use of profit, same problems of insider advantage and incentive. |
