# Case 13: Normally Distributed Violence (detailed review)

## 1. The case at a glance

| Fact | Source in the case |
| --- | --- |
| "Stochastic terrorism": an influential speaker spreads rhetoric that statistically raises the likelihood of violence, usually by a lone actor the speaker doesn't know | Amman and Meloy, *Perspectives on Terrorism*, 2021 |
| It works through hostile, dehumanizing, escalating rhetoric; it inspires but does not direct; the attack is "statistically predictable but individually unpredictable" | Same |
| Henry II asked whether no one would rid him of a "meddlesome priest"; four knights then killed the Archbishop of Canterbury without his order | Case text |
| With audiences of hundreds of millions, a tiny extremist share can produce serious violence | Case text |
| A probability of crime is not a crime, and suggesting is not directing | Case text |
| For liability: dehumanizing rhetoric raises the odds; large audiences give outsized influence; lack of immediacy gives plausible deniability | Case text |
| Against: liability carves exceptions into free expression, which protects diverse views and even exaggeration and lies | Case text |
| *Whitney v. California* (1927) upheld punishing some harmful speech; *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) protected speech unless directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it | 274 U.S. 357; 395 U.S. 444 |
| Hypothetical: a popular figure dehumanizes a group at a rally and calls for action to impede a political event happening within hours, nearby. Is that "imminent"? | Case text |

**What the case doesn't say:** it gives no example of a lone attacker citing a specific speaker, and no data on how often rhetoric precedes violence. Avoid claiming causation (Dr. B's post hoc warning).

**Outside the case (verify before use):** in 2003 the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda convicted founders of radio station RTLM and the editor of *Kangura* of direct and public incitement to genocide. *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) requires at least recklessness to prosecute true threats. Henry II did public penance at Canterbury in 1174.

## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** holding speakers accountable for violence that runs through other people's free choices *versus* protecting expression from a probability standard that whoever holds power could turn on critics.

**The traps.** First, the case blends moral responsibility with legal liability; our answer differs for each, so say which you mean. Second, the closing hypothetical is close to classic incitement, not stochastic terrorism; treat it separately.

| Who | Stake | Voice in the case? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Targeted groups and individuals | Safety, dignity, equal standing | Barely |
| Influential speakers | Freedom to speak hard truths | Yes |
| The large non-violent audience | Access to political speech | No |
| The lone actor | Remains fully responsible | Implicit |
| Platforms that amplify | Profit from engagement; shape reach | No |
| Future dissenters and protest movements | Most at risk if "dangerous rhetoric" becomes punishable | No |

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| Are those who spread hateful rhetoric responsible when it inspires others to commit violence? (Dr. B's version) | Yes, morally, in proportion to content, reach, foreseeability and persistence, without reducing the attacker's responsibility. |
| Should stochastic terrorism be a crime? | No, not as a probability-based offense; keep the law at incitement to imminent violence. |
| Is the rally in the hypothetical "imminent"? | Very likely: a call to act against a specific event, nearby, within hours, to an inflamed crowd is directed and likely, so it's incitement. |
| Does intent matter? | It aggravates responsibility, but recklessness about a known risk is enough. |

**Our thesis:** Speakers who spread dehumanizing rhetoric share moral responsibility for the violence it inspires, scaled by content, reach, foreseeability and persistence, without reducing the attacker's guilt; legal liability should stay limited to incitement to imminent violence, because a probability standard in law would be wielded by whoever holds power.

## 4. Claim 1: Speakers of dehumanizing rhetoric share moral responsibility for violence it inspires.

**Logic line:** Because you can be responsible for harm that passes through someone else's free choice when you knowingly raise its risk (S1), a tiny chance times a huge audience is a serious risk (S2), and dehumanizing rhetoric is morally different from harsh opinion (S3), these speakers share responsibility.

**S1. Responsibility isn't a fixed pie.** The attacker bears all of their own guilt; the speaker bears separate guilt for raising the risk, like a getaway driver.
*Backing (law, history):* accomplice liability holds people who knowingly encourage a crime responsible alongside the person who commits it. Henry II never ordered Becket's murder, yet did public penance in 1174.

**S2. Moral mathematics.** One in a million, times ten million listeners, is about ten people.
*Backing (framework and analogy):* Derek Parfit's "mistakes in moral mathematics" (1984): it's a mistake to ignore small chances of great harm spread across many people. We blame a drunk driver for the risk they impose even on nights they don't crash. The speaker controls the curve, not which point on it acts.

**S3. Dehumanization is an act, not just an opinion.**
*Backing (frameworks):* Kant's humanity formula (the team's lead framework): the speaker treats listeners as tools, betting one will act, and targets as obstacles. Jeremy Waldron's *The Harm in Hate Speech* (2012): it attacks a group's assurance of equal standing. Austin's speech-act theory: labeling a group as vermin is an act of marking them. The 2003 Rwanda media convictions show rhetoric can be a recognized cause of mass violence.

**Ethical analysis.** Judge *function, not viewpoint*: does the speech present a group as subhuman, a disease, or an enemy to be removed? Sartre's "bad faith" (Dr. B's suggestion) names plausible deniability: knowing your audience hears Y while saying "I only meant X."
**We concede:** the attacker is always the primary wrongdoer, and responsibility can't be measured precisely.

## 5. Claim 2: Responsibility scales with content, reach, foreseeability and persistence, but legal liability should stay at incitement to imminent violence.

**Logic line:** Because speakers can prevent misuse and choose their words (S1), foreseeability grows once violence has followed (S2), and the power to punish "dangerous rhetoric" is itself dangerous (S3), moral responsibility scales while legal liability stays narrow.

**S1. Speakers can prevent misuse.**
*Backing (examples from practice):* Martin Luther King Jr. paired mass audiences with explicit, repeated commitments to nonviolence. Major speakers have speechwriters and rehearse; "I can't control how people interpret me" is weak from someone who crafts every line.

**S2. The second time is never random.**
*Backing (law):* *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) uses recklessness for true threats; moral responsibility needs no more. Once a speaker learns violence followed their words and continues, it's foreseeable.

**S3. The law must stay narrow.**
*Backing (law and history):* Brandeis in *Whitney*: if there's time to answer falsehood through discussion, the remedy is more speech. Protection cuts both ways: *Whitney* convicted a left-wing activist; *Brandenburg* protected a Klan leader. Civil-rights leaders were routinely accused of stirring up violence.

**Ethical analysis.** Second-order effects of a probability-based crime: selective enforcement against whoever is out of power, a coded-language arms race (dog whistles adapt faster than statutes), and a chill on legitimate anger at injustice. That's why norms, platforms and counter-speech carry most of the weight.
**We concede:** dog whistles make the content test hard at the margins, and "stochastic terrorism" as a label can itself be weaponized.

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "The listener chose. Blaming the speaker excuses the attacker, and you can't prove causation" (post hoc, Dr. B).**
*Their supports:* (1) autonomy; (2) B after A doesn't mean A caused B; (3) the case gives no causal data.
*Our response:* **Concede** (2) and (3) for individual cases. **Rebut** (1): responsibility isn't a fixed pie. **Distinguish:** our claim is about raising risk, which is wrong even when no single attack can be traced, like drunk driving; where we assert a link, we use evidence such as a manifesto.

**Counterclaim: "Your test sweeps in legitimate policy debate" (raised in practice: George Wallace, the "welfare queen" trope).**
*Our response:* a policy position, even an odious one, isn't dehumanization by itself, but context drives foreseeability (Alabama in 1963, with churches being bombed).

**Counterclaim: "This is a partisan attack dressed up as ethics."**
*Our response:* run the flip test aloud: our four factors don't mention politics. Keep a second example from a different side ready.
