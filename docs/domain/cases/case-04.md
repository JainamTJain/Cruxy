## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** personal freedom to improve one's appearance *versus* a culture that turns appearance into a moral duty and a market that profits from insecurity.

**The trap:** the case asks about a *duty*, but most of its evidence is about *benefits*. A benefit does not create a duty. Also, don't argue against self-improvement as such, or a judge will ask about haircuts and braces.

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| Do we have a duty to be beautiful? (the case's question) | No: we have no moral duty to be beautiful, though caring for one's appearance can be a permissible personal choice. |
| Is looksmaxxing morally permissible? | Softmaxxing is permissible self-care; marketing extreme practices to minors and exploiting insecurity are wrong. |
| Is pursuing beauty rational given its benefits? | Individually sometimes, but rational is not the same as obligatory, and the collective race makes everyone worse off. |
| Who is responsible for lookism? | Institutions that reward looks (hiring, courts, schools) carry a duty to reduce that bias. |

**Our thesis:** There is no duty to be beautiful; pursuing appearance is a permissible personal choice when it is free and safe, but turning beauty into a moral standard, and profiting by selling extreme practices to young people, is wrong.

## 4. Claim 1: We have no moral duty to be beautiful.

**Logic line:** Because duties attach to what we control and what we owe others (S1), treating beauty as a moral standard punishes people for traits they didn't choose (S2), and a benefit to the individual is not an obligation (S3), there is no duty to be beautiful.

**S1. Duties track control and what we owe others.** "Ought implies can": morality can't require what people can't do, and much of appearance comes from genetics, illness or disability.
*Backing (framework):* Kant's principle that ought implies can. Our team raised illness and genetics in practice.

**S2. Making beauty a moral ideal produces unjust judgments.**
*Backing (case fact):* Heather Widdows shows people are praised or shamed by how they look, and the case notes beauty ideals link weight or untidiness to laziness. The research in the case shows looks shape earnings and even criminal judgments, which are outcomes people don't deserve.

**S3. Benefit is not duty.** That something pays off doesn't mean we owe it to anyone.
*Backing (example):* knowing three languages helps a career, but no one has a moral duty to be trilingual. The case's proponents show pursuing beauty is rational, not obligatory.

**Ethical analysis.** The values in tension are self-determination and equal respect. Simone de Beauvoir's distinction between subject and object (raised by the team) explains the harm: a duty to be beautiful makes people objects to be judged rather than subjects who act.
**We concede:** basic hygiene and courtesy in shared spaces are reasonable social expectations, but they're about respect for others, not beauty.

## 5. Claim 2: Caring for one's appearance is a permissible choice, but marketing extreme practices to young people and profiting from insecurity is wrong.

**Logic line:** Because autonomous adults may shape their own bodies (S1), minors and insecure audiences can't consent freely to a market built on their anxieties (S2), and each person's maxxing raises the bar for everyone (S3), the practice is permissible as personal choice but its predatory promotion is not.

**S1. Autonomy covers self-care.** Grooming, fitness and even elective procedures can express a person's own goals.
*Backing (framework):* Kant's respect for autonomy; the team's working claim: "pursuing beauty is permissible on autonomy grounds."

**S2. Consent requires freedom from manipulation and enough maturity.** The movement mainly targets adolescents.
*Backing (framework and case fact):* soft paternalism allows protecting people whose choices aren't fully informed or voluntary, such as minors. Our team raised minors using untested weight-loss drugs and influencers promoting unhealthy standards.

**S3. Looks work like an arms race.** When everyone invests to look better than others, the standard rises and no one gains relative advantage, but everyone pays.
*Backing (example):* the team's "Instagram face" point: aesthetics converge even without explicit promotion. Economists call this a positional good: its value depends on being ahead of others.

**Ethical analysis.** Care ethics asks what we owe the young and insecure, and virtue ethics asks whether a life organized around one's face shows a well-ordered character. Both support limits on predatory promotion, not on personal choice.
**We concede:** where to draw the line between "extreme" and "elective" is contested; we draw it at high health risk and at targeting minors.

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "Pursuing beauty is rational and even prudent, because attractiveness brings real advantages."**
*Their supports:* (1) higher earnings, (2) better treatment by courts and employers, (3) social and romantic success (all from the case's research).
*Our response:* **Concede** the advantages are real. **Distinguish** prudence from duty (Claim 1, S3). **Rebut** the conclusion: the right response to unjust advantages is to reduce the bias in institutions, not to demand that everyone chase the ideal.

**Counterclaim: "Criticizing men's looksmaxxing is a double standard; women have done this openly for decades."**
*Our response:* **Concede** the double standard in the critics, as the case notes. Our claims apply equally to all genders: no one has a duty to be beautiful, and no one should be sold extreme practices as a minor.
