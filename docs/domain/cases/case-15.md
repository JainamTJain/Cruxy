## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** the moral significance of animals' capacity to suffer *versus* a legal and economic system built on owning and using them.

**The trap:** "personhood" means different things. Moral worth, membership in the moral community, and moral agency are different (Dr. B's distinction). Animals can have moral worth without being moral agents who can have duties.

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| Should animals continue to be treated as property? | Not as mere property: sentient animals have moral worth and should have legal protection from unjustified harm, though not full personhood. |
| Should animals have legal personhood? | Not full personhood, but standing to be protected, with others able to act on their behalf. |
| Is factory farming ethical? | No: it causes severe suffering that isn't necessary for human survival or basic welfare. |

**Our thesis:** Sentient animals have moral worth, so the law should treat them as more than property: harm to them is permissible only when necessary and minimized, which condemns factory farming, while full legal personhood is neither needed nor coherent for beings who can't hold duties.

## 4. Claim 1: Sentient animals have moral worth, so treating them as mere property is wrong.

**Logic line:** Because the capacity to suffer is what makes suffering matter morally (S1), science and law increasingly recognize animal sentience (S2), and property law ignores that difference (S3), mere property status is wrong.

**S1. Suffering matters wherever it occurs.**
*Backing (framework):* Peter Singer's equal consideration of interests: a being's pain counts whoever's it is. Our team: sentience is sufficient for moral worth, though not necessary.

**S2. Sentience is recognized.**
*Backing (case facts):* Birch et al. (2020) on assessing sentience; Germany, New Zealand, Denmark, Spain and the UK recognize it in law.

**S3. Property law treats a dog like a car.**
*Backing (case example):* Regan's car, dog and stray: under property law the harm to a dog is a harm to its owner, so a stray has no protection at all.

**Ethical analysis.** We distinguish moral worth (counting morally), moral community (being owed duties) and moral agency (having duties). Animals have the first two, not the third. Analogy: children and comatose patients are owed protection without being moral agents.
**We concede:** not all animals are equally sentient, and nociception (a reflex to harm) differs from conscious pain; the science is incomplete at the margins.

## 5. Claim 2: Harm to animals is permissible only when necessary and minimized, which condemns factory farming but doesn't require full personhood.

**Logic line:** Because "ought implies can" and human needs are real (S1), legal standing can protect beings who can't speak for themselves (S2), and factory farming fails the necessity test (S3), we support protection from unjustified harm, not full personhood.

**S1. Transitional, realistic ethics.**
*Backing (framework):* "ought implies can": morality can't demand instant abolition of all animal use. Our team's principle: harm only if necessary and minimal, with a transition over time.

**S2. Standing without personhood.**
*Backing (outside example):* Christopher Stone's "Should Trees Have Standing?" (1972) argued that natural objects could have legal guardians; the "sentient property" middle position works the same way.

**S3. Factory farming fails the test.**
*Backing (team position and framework):* the suffering is severe and mostly unnecessary for human survival. Even Kant, who denied animals direct moral status, held that cruelty to animals damages our humanity toward people.

**Ethical analysis.** Utilitarian (Singer) and rights-based (Regan) views disagree on much but converge on condemning factory farming. Richard Posner's skepticism (from our sources) is the main counterweight: human interests come first. Our necessity test answers it: human interests can win when they're real needs.
**We concede:** where to draw "necessary" is contested, for example for medical research or subsistence hunting.

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "Animals should have full legal personhood, like corporations or rivers."**
*Their supports:* (1) property status invites abuse; (2) corporations and rivers already have legal personhood; (3) India's dolphin designation.
*Our response:* **Concede** (1). **Distinguish** (2): legal personhood for corporations is a tool for holding rights and duties; animals can't hold duties, so standing and guardianship fit better. **Rebut** the implication that personhood alone fixes suffering: enforcement and standards matter more.

**Counterclaim: "Animals are property; welfare laws are enough."**
*Our response:* Regan's stray example shows property law leaves some animals with no protection, and factory farming shows welfare laws are weak.
