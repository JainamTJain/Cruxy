## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** lives saved by money from a tainted source *versus* the integrity of a mission and the trust it depends on.

**The trap:** a false dilemma between "take all money" and "refuse all tainted money." Also separate two questions: was the *money* made through wrongdoing, or is the *donor* personally compromised?

| Partner organizations | Reputation, mission | Yes, by withdrawing |
| Epstein's victims | Recognition; not being erased by "good works" | No |

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| On what moral grounds should a nonprofit deny funds from organizations that undermine its mission? (Dr. B's version) | When the funds were produced by the wrong, when accepting them signals approval of it, or when the association would destroy the trust the mission depends on. |
| Should partners keep working with the Gates Foundation? | Yes, with conditions: the money wasn't produced by the wrongdoing, but partners should demand transparency and distance from the individual. |
| How do damning revelations complicate weighing consequences? | They add consequences people miss: lost trust, and a message that wealth can buy moral cover. |

**Our thesis:** Nonprofits should refuse money produced by the wrong or given to launder a reputation, but money from a compromised donor can be accepted when it wasn't produced by the wrongdoing and the organization protects its independence and the trust of the people it serves.

## 4. Claim 1: Tainted money can be accepted when it wasn't produced by the wrong, because refusing it costs lives and repairs nothing.

**Logic line:** Because the HPV program saves lives that refusal would cost (S1), money's moral status depends on how it was made more than who holds it (S2), and refusing doesn't help the wronged (S3), partners may continue, with conditions.

**S1. Lives are at stake.**
*Backing (case fact):* 350,000 deaths a year from cervical cancer; about 4 million girls vaccinated.

**S2. Distinguish the source of the money from the conduct of the donor.**
*Backing (team idea):* our practice distinguished crime that produced the money from crime separate from it. The foundation's money wasn't made through the conduct revealed in the documents.

**S3. Refusal helps no victim.**
*Backing (example, outside the case):* restorative alternatives exist: the Epstein victims' fund compensated survivors directly. Partners can support repair rather than walk away from patients.

**Ethical analysis.** Consequences matter here, but pair them with principle: María Lugones's idea of impurity (from our team's notes) holds that moral life is rarely pure, and insisting on purity can be a luxury paid for by the vulnerable.
**We concede:** the program's success doesn't erase the wrongdoing, and Gates's apology doesn't settle the matter.

## 5. Claim 2: Nonprofits must refuse money that launders reputation or destroys the trust their mission depends on.

**Logic line:** Because charity can be used to buy moral cover (S1), public health programs depend on community trust (S2), and accepting money is a public act that sends a message (S3), organizations must protect their independence and sometimes refuse.

**S1. Reputation laundering is real.** Giving can be used to change a public story about a donor.
*Backing (framework):* Kant's humanity formula: using a charity's beneficiaries as a means to repair one's image treats them as tools.

**S2. Health programs run on trust.**
*Backing (case fact and outside example):* the 2013 email about paying Afghan tribal leaders shows how quickly a vaccination drive's methods can become a scandal; the CIA's fake vaccination campaign in Pakistan damaged trust in vaccines for years (verify).

**S3. Acceptance is expressive.**
*Backing (team idea):* donor anonymity and transparency rules: an organization can accept funds without naming rights, publish its conditions, and keep decision-making independent.

**Ethical analysis.** Leif Wenar's critique of aid (from our notes) adds that aid should be accountable to the communities it serves, not just to donors. Virtue ethics asks what an organization of integrity does: it doesn't refuse all imperfect money, but it doesn't sell its name either.
**We concede:** the line between "accepting with conditions" and "laundering" is a judgment call.

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "Partners must cut ties; taking the money makes them complicit and silences victims."**
*Their supports:* (1) association signals approval, (2) victims deserve solidarity, (3) other funders exist.
*Our response:* **Concede** (1) and (2) matter, which is why Claim 2 requires conditions and distance. **Rebut** (3): the case doesn't show other funders would replace the money, and girls who miss vaccination pay that cost. **Distinguish:** walking away from Gates personally is different from walking away from the patients.

**Counterclaim: "Only outcomes matter; the source is irrelevant."**
*Our response:* outcomes include trust; Claim 2 shows lost trust can cost lives too.
