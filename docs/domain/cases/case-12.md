## 2. Crux, trap and stakeholders

**Crux:** protecting people from dangerous health falsehoods *versus* letting a party with its own interests decide what is true.

**The trap:** Article 13 is about *expertise* (who may present medical science); Article 4 is about *ideology* (who holds "correct" values). A team that treats them as one either defends ideological screening or condemns expertise checks. Name the split in the first minute.

| Who | Stake | Voice in the case? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patients and parents seeking health information | Safety, access to useful knowledge | Indirect |
| Credentialed clinicians | Authority, but exposure to political pressure | Partial |
| Uncredentialed sharers (patients, caregivers, traditional-medicine communities) | Being heard; lived knowledge | Barely |
| Platforms | Liability, verification costs, over-removal | Partial |
| The state | Public health, and its own interest in what counts as "true" | Yes |
| Children, the elderly, people with low health literacy | Most exposed, least able to check | No |

## 3. Moderator questions and our answer

| Possible question | Our one-sentence answer |
| --- | --- |
| Under what circumstances, if any, may a government regulate expression to curb misinformation? (Dr. B's version) | Only when a checkable claim, presented as expertise or used to sell, risks serious physical harm, and then with the mildest tool that works, applied by a body independent of political power. |
| Is it permissible for China to require credentials to post medical content? | Requiring disclosure of credentials, yes; requiring credentials before anyone may speak about health, no; Article 4's values test, never. |
| Who should decide what counts as medical misinformation? | An expert body independent of political power, with published standards and appeal. |
| Is labeling just soft censorship? | No: a label adds information and leaves the choice with the reader. |

**Our thesis:** A government may regulate health misinformation only within four conditions (a checkable claim; presented as expertise or used to sell; serious physical harm; least restrictive tool applied by an independent decider); that justifies credential labels and corrections, not licensing all health talk, and never a "correct values" test.

## 4. Claim 1: Some health speech may be regulated: checkable, false claims presented as expertise or used to sell, which risk serious physical harm.

**Logic line:** Because the state may limit speech to prevent serious harm to others (S1), some health claims clearly cause such harm (S2), and borrowing medical authority creates duties ordinary conversation doesn't have (S3), narrow regulation of those claims is justified.

**S1. The harm principle, narrowly.** Limit freedom only to prevent serious harm to others, and only by the least restrictive means that works.
*Backing (framework and law):* Mill's harm principle; Article 19(3) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which China signed in 1998, allows limits necessary for public health.

**S2. The harm is real and large.**
*Backing (case fact and outside fact):* 92% of 1.07 billion short-video users consume health content; the U.S. had 2,065 measles cases in 2025.

**S3. Borrowed authority carries duties.** No one can check everything, so we must trust experts we can't verify.
*Backing (framework):* C. Thi Nguyen on trust (from our resources): faking expertise exploits a trust we can't avoid giving. The grammar test separates claims ("X causes Y," "do this to cure Z") from anecdotes ("I tried this") and values ("trust natural remedies").

**Ethical analysis.** The values in tension are free inquiry and protection from harm. Bad vaccine advice harms third parties (infants, the immunocompromised), which takes it out of purely self-regarding choice.
**We concede:** the line between claims and values has borderline cases; hard cases fall on the protected side by default.

## 5. Claim 2: Credential labels and corrections are justified; licensing all health talk is not, and Article 4's values test never is.

**Logic line:** Because medical knowledge is fallible and often corrected by outsiders (S1), whoever decides what is false must not have a stake in the answer (S2), and labels inform without silencing (S3), the right tool is labels and corrections by an independent body.

**S1. Fallibility.** Silencing an opinion assumes we can't be wrong.
*Backing (framework and history):* Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. 2. Semmelweis's handwashing was rejected in the 1840s; the bacterial cause of ulcers won a 2005 Nobel after doubt; Li Wenliang's warning was labeled a rumor. Miranda Fricker's epistemic injustice: credential gates can discount patients' and traditional communities' knowledge (the team's Flonase example).

**S2. No stake in the answer.**
*Backing (case fact):* Article 4 makes political orientation a condition of speaking. Facebook's 2021 reversal shows labels shift in democracies too. Flip test: would you give this power to officials you distrust?

**S3. Labels pass; licensing fails.**
*Backing (team idea and outside fact):* credential tags let viewers judge; Google already holds "Your Money or Your Life" pages to a higher bar. Licensing fails because credentials don't guarantee truth (Purdue's 2007 plea).

**Ethical analysis.** Labels are soft paternalism at most: they inform without overriding. Amplification is not speech: platforms carry duties for what their algorithms push, but heavy liability makes them over-remove (Jack Balkin's "collateral censorship"), so pair duties with appeal rights.
**We concede:** labels won't move people who already distrust institutions; independence is a matter of degree.

## 6. Counterclaims and our responses

**Counterclaim: "Labels and media literacy are too little, too late" (the case's own closing line).**
*Their supports:* (1) measles spreads faster than literacy campaigns; (2) labels are ignored; (3) countries that restrict more seem fine.
*Our response:* **Concede** (1): our tool is the least restrictive one *that works*, so demotion in feeds acts immediately, and repeated or commercial harmful claims can be removed. **Rebut** (3): comparisons mix many causes. **Distinguish:** we reject licensing everyone to stop the few.

**Counterclaim: "It's like shouting fire in a crowded theater."**
*Our response:* that line comes from *Schenck* (1919), which jailed an anti-draft leafleter and was later replaced by the *Brandenburg* test. The better analogy is fraud: a false cure sold for profit is regulable because of the sale and the harm.

**Counterclaim: "You're judging China by American norms."**
*Our response:* our test comes from the ICCPR, which China signed. And we argue the case as written (Dr. B), critiquing Article 4 as the case presents it.

## 8. Judge question bank

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| If an influencer's posts are linked to children's deaths, why not ban them? | Repeated, harmful, checkable claims can be removed under our test; we reject licensing everyone to stop the few. |
| Isn't requiring credentials to post like requiring a medical license? | No: a license governs one-to-one treatment; posting is public speech, where a label protects at less cost. |
| Who decides when science is still evolving? | An independent body with published standards and appeal; open questions aren't "checkable" at all. |
| A licensed doctor posts dangerous falsehoods. What then? | Our test targets the claim, not the credential, so it applies. |
| Would you trust current U.S. health officials with this power? | Our claim doesn't depend on trusting any official; the independence condition makes it acceptable across politics. |
| What should actually happen in China? | Keep credential disclosure, drop credential requirements for general health talk, move truth decisions to an independent panel with appeal, remove Article 4's political test. |
| What about AI-generated health content? | Same claim-based test, plus disclosure that it's AI-generated, and registry-backed credentials. |
