# Part 1. Start here

Read these lines first. Each one is a single instruction.

### How we prepare a case

1. Every case has two leads, one counter-arguer and one supporter.
2. Leads know the case cold: every fact, every source, every number.
3. The counter-arguer builds the strongest case against us and our answer to it.
4. The supporter writes and gives the intro and the closing.
5. Everyone reads every case, even the ones they don't own.
6. Start with the detailed review in this doc, then write the one-pager from it.
7. Every case needs at least two claims of our own.
8. Every claim needs three supports.
9. Every support needs backing: a case fact, a source, an example, or a framework explained in plain words.
10. Every case needs at least one counterclaim, stated fairly, with our response.
11. Write each claim in one sentence. If it takes three, it isn't ready.
12. Check every number before anyone says it out loud.
13. Explain any theory in one plain sentence, or leave it out.
14. Run a timed presentation and a judges' Q&A before calling a case done.

### How we compete

1. Answer the moderator's question in the first 30 seconds.
2. Say what we concede before anyone asks.
3. Name the strongest objection before the other team does, then answer it.
4. Keep answers to judges under 45 seconds, then stop.
5. When we comment on the other team, raise their one or two most important gaps, not ten small ones.
6. Disagree with ideas, never with people.
7. Bring nothing to the table but blank scratch paper: outside notes are not allowed.
8. Stay ethical, not legal: if a judge pushes for a policy or a law, answer briefly, then bring it back to the moral question.

### How we know a case is ready

1. Both leads can give the 10-minute version without notes.
2. The counter-arguer has tried to break each claim, and the claim survived.
3. We have answered every question in the judge bank out loud.
4. Someone outside the team has judged one practice round on it.

# Part 2. Roles and round setup

Each case has four owners who build its materials. In a match we don't choose the case, so whoever sits at the table must be able to run any case. Owners make that possible.

| Role | Owns in prep | Speaks in our presentation | In judges' Q&A | When we comment on the other team |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lead 1: argument architect | Claims, supports, frameworks | Claim 1 with its three supports | Questions about our principle and frameworks | Drafts commentary on their principle |
| Lead 2: case expert | Every fact, number and source; the crux; consistency with other cases | Claim 2 with its three supports | Factual and "what about X?" questions | Spots their factual gaps |
| Counter-arguer | The counterclaims, attacks on each support, our concessions | The counterclaim, stated fairly, and our response | "But isn't that...?" challenges | Leads our commentary |
| Supporter | Intro, roadmap, closing, timing, why it matters | Intro and closing | Restates our position if an answer drifts | Opens and closes our commentary |
| Other seats | Question-bank drills, fact checks | None | Passes one-line notes on scratch paper | Tracks their claims on scratch paper |

**Timing for a 10-minute presentation.** Supporter 0:00 to 0:45 (answer the question, give the roadmap). Lead 1 0:45 to 4:00 (Claim 1). Lead 2 4:00 to 7:15 (Claim 2). Counter-arguer 7:15 to 9:15 (counterclaim and response). Supporter 9:15 to 10:00 (closing). National rules allow 7 to 12 minutes, so confirm the California length and scale these blocks.

**Picking owners.** Every competitor owns at least one case. Leads are the two people who will know it deepest. The counter-arguer should be someone who first disagreed with the team's claim.

**Building round lineups.** We have 14 competitors rotating across rounds. Check each lineup against the case list: every case should have at least one of its owners at the table. If a case has no owner seated, brief that table on it the night before.

**During the match.** Only seated members may speak, and only one person speaks at a time during conferral. Unseated teammates and coaches may not communicate with the table during a match.

# Part 3. Rules and judging

Everything in this part matches the documents in our Drive's Training Documents folder (the Guide to Scoring, the Fillable Scoring Sheet and the Etiquette FAQ) and APPE's current regional rules.

## The scoring sheet

Each judge fills in this sheet for both teams. Every item is scored 1 to 10, and spirit points are scored separately from 1 to 5.

| Item | What the judge asks | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Presentation A | Was the presentation clear and systematic? | 1 to 10 |
| Presentation B | Did it clearly identify and thoroughly discuss the central moral dimensions of the case? | 1 to 10 |
| Presentation C | Did it show awareness of, and thoughtful consideration for, different viewpoints, especially those that would weigh heavily for people who disagree? | 1 to 10 |
| Presentation total | A + B + C | 3 to 30 |
| Commentary | Our commentary on the other team's presentation | 1 to 10 |
| Response to commentary | Our reply to their commentary | 1 to 10 |
| Response to judges | Our answers in Q&A | 1 to 10 |
| Team total | Presentation + commentary + response + judges | Up to 60 |
| Spirit points | Respectful, collegial engagement (feedback, separate sheet) | 1 to 5 |

**What this means for us.** Half of the presentation score (item C) and a third of the whole sheet reward engaging opposing views. That is why every case in this doc has a counterclaim built in.

## Score bands

| Score | Meaning | Grade equivalent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | Exceptional | A+ |
| 9 | Outstanding | A |
| 8 | Very solid job | B+ / A- |
| 7 | Solid job | B |
| 6 | Noticeable weaknesses or omissions | C+ / B |
| 5 | More serious weaknesses or omissions | C- / C |
| 3 to 4 | Seriously flawed or largely absent | D |
| 1 to 2 | Almost completely or completely absent | F |

## What judges look for in each part (Guide to Scoring)

**Presentation: clarity and organization.** Make the answer to the moderator's question clear. Keep the discussion easy to follow. Explain terms so an educated person could follow. Don't talk over each other. Judges may not score attire, appearance, volume, eye contact, or how many of us speak.

**Presentation: ethical analysis.** Show clear ethical reasoning that supports our position, and keep it logically plausible. Theory is optional; if we use it, explain it, and it only counts if it deepens understanding of the case. Research isn't scored for being there, only for what it adds. Judges must separate a logical flaw from disagreement with our conclusion.

**Presentation: alternative viewpoints.** Show there may be more than one set of reasons for our view, and reasons for other views. Don't just mention objections: show we appreciate their merits and respond thoughtfully. We don't have to announce "now the objections."

**Commentary.** Show we listened carefully: respond to their points or note what they left out. Make a good-faith effort to understand their position accurately. Speak as a collegial conversation. Separate the most pressing issues from peripheral ones. We don't have to disagree with them.

**Response to commentary.** Address the points we judge most pressing; we don't have to answer every point. Judges decide whether we handled the most pressing ones. Stay collegial.

**Judges' Q&A.** Judges have up to one minute to confer, then a ten-minute Q&A. Each judge gets one question and one follow-up, and more if time remains. We may confer before answering. Give full but concise answers. A question that sounds like disagreement doesn't mean the judge disagrees or that our score dropped. Stronger presentations get tougher questions.

## What judges are told to ask (Guide to Asking Judges' Questions)

Prepare for every one of these question types on every case:

- Follow-ups on issues we skipped or treated too briefly.
- Pushback on our own position, asking us to engage the strongest counterargument.
- Points the commentary team raised, asked again by a judge.
- "Change the facts" hypotheticals: how does our view change if one fact changes?
- "State your view and your main reasons," especially if our presentation was unclear.

## The regional rules (2026)

- Presentations may run 7 to 12 minutes, commentary 5 to 8 minutes, and judges' Q&A 10 to 15 minutes; regions set the exact times.
- Response to commentary may be shortened to lengthen Q&A.
- Regions may allow one minute of initial conferral.
- Scratch paper is allowed; pre-written notes and outside notes are not.
- Unseated members and coaches may not communicate with the seated team during a match.
- Unless a region varies it, a match is won by a majority of judges; regions may instead use total points.
- Time expiring is a hard stop by default ("that's time").

## Etiquette (Etiquette FAQ)

- Ethics Bowl is an educational event built on respectful dialogue, listening and democratic deliberation.
- Constructive disagreement is welcome; personal attacks, dismissiveness and hostility are not.
- During the other team's turn: no loud whispering, faces, inappropriate laughter, or tactics meant to belittle them.
- If scoring feels unfair, stay respectful in the room; concerns go privately to organizers through Dr. B.

## What we learned watching the national finals (our APPE Nationals Notes)

- The first speaker restated the question, answered it, and handed off to the point person.
- They grounded the answer in named principles ("we ground this in two principles") and defined the key term in the question.
- They used four pieces of evidence, then returned to the point person to recap the main argument.
- A teammate was handed the counterpoint explicitly: "I'm going to hand off to a teammate for a counterpoint."
- They used everyday analogies (we don't harvest organs from the brain dead; a driver's license organ-donor choice) and a precedent from the case set.
- If another team cites a philosopher without explaining them, respond: "We haven't read X, but we think you're asking..." and answer the substance.

## Coach decisions from practice (Dr. B)

- Use claim, evidence, reason. Judges usually don't have philosophy backgrounds, so don't present formal premise-premise-conclusion logic; present plain claims with supports.
- This is ethics bowl, not legal bowl or policy bowl. Law can inform, but redirect legal or policy questions back to the ethics.
- Explain every framework we name, or we will be marked down.
- Don't rely on utilitarianism alone: outcomes are easy to cherry-pick. Pair a principle with consequences.
- Argue the case as written, even if a source says something different.
- Think at two levels: what institutions or governments should do, and what individuals and communities can do.

# Part 4. How we build an argument

## Why cogency is our standard

Ethical arguments are rarely airtight proofs. An argument is **cogent** when its reasons are believable, relevant and, taken together, strong enough that a fair-minded listener should find the conclusion more reasonable than the alternatives. That is exactly what the scoring guide asks for: analysis that is "logically plausible" and that engages opposing views on their merits.

Use the **ARS test** on every support (from informal logic):

- **Acceptable:** would a fair-minded judge accept this reason as true or reasonable? If not, back it with a source or example.
- **Relevant:** does it actually bear on the claim, or just sound good next to it?
- **Sufficient:** together, do the three supports carry enough weight for the claim as stated? If not, narrow the claim.

## The structure every case follows

> Each case has at least two claims of our own. Each claim has three supports. Each support has backing. Each case has at least one counterclaim with our response.

**A claim** is one sentence that answers the moderator's question, with its conditions if any.

**A support** is a reason the claim is true. Three is the target: one principle, one piece of evidence about the world, and one that connects the principle to this case works well.

**Backing** is what makes a support believable. Use one of four kinds:

| Backing type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Case fact | "The case reports 92% of China's short-video users consume health content." |
| Outside source | A study, court decision or news report, marked as outside the case and verified. |
| Example or analogy | "We don't harvest organs from the brain dead." |
| Framework in plain words | "Kant: never treat people merely as tools for someone else's goals." |

**The logic line.** Write every claim as one simple line: "Because S1, S2 and S3, therefore the claim." If a listener could accept S1, S2 and S3 and still reject the claim, a support is missing.

**A counterclaim** is the strongest opposing view, stated so its supporters would agree with how we put it, with its own supports. Then our response, which does one of three things: **concede** part of it, **distinguish** our case from it, or **rebut** a support.

## Ethical analysis: what goes under every claim

- **Values in tension:** name the two goods that pull against each other (for example, safety versus free expression).
- **Stakeholders:** who gains, who loses, and who has no voice in the case.
- **Frameworks as lenses:** use one or two, explained in one sentence each, and say where they fall short.
- **Principle plus consequences:** never argue from outcomes alone or rules alone.
- **Concessions:** what we grant, said before anyone asks.

## Stress tests before a claim is final

- **Counterexample:** a case where our principle gives a bad answer. How do we handle it?
- **Flip test:** swap the country, the politics or the side. Do we still hold the claim?
- **Slope test:** where does the principle stop, and why does it stop there?
- **Second-order effects:** what happens a step or two after our answer is adopted?
- **Change the facts:** which single fact, if changed, would change our answer? (Judges are told to ask this.)

## How the structure maps onto a 10-minute presentation

| Time | Speaker | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 to 0:45 | Supporter | Restate the question, answer it in one sentence, preview Claim 1 and Claim 2 |
| 0:45 to 4:00 | Lead 1 | Claim 1: logic line, three supports with backing, one line of ethical analysis |
| 4:00 to 7:15 | Lead 2 | Claim 2: logic line, three supports with backing, one line of ethical analysis |
| 7:15 to 9:15 | Counter-arguer | Counterclaim at full strength, then concede, distinguish or rebut |
| 9:15 to 10:00 | Supporter | Recap both claims, what we concede, why it matters |

## Frameworks quick reference

| Framework | One sentence | Best for | Where it falls short |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kant, humanity formula | Never treat people merely as tools; always also as ends in themselves | Exploitation, consent, dignity | Hard to weigh against large consequences |
| Kant, universalizability | Act only on a rule everyone could follow without contradiction | Free-riding, double standards | Rules can be described to pass the test |
| Consequentialism (Mill, Singer) | Judge actions by their outcomes for everyone affected | Scale, harm versus benefit | Outcomes are uncertain and easy to cherry-pick |
| Mill's harm principle | Limit freedom only to prevent harm to others | Speech, liberty, regulation | "Harm" can be stretched or shrunk |
| Virtue ethics (Aristotle) | Ask what a person of good character would do | Personal and professional choices | Gives less precise answers |
| Care ethics (Gilligan, Noddings) | Relationships and vulnerability come first | Minors, dependency, community | Hard to scale to institutions |
| Rawls, veil of ignorance | Design rules without knowing who you'll be | Fairness, access | Critics say it imagines able-bodied, ideal deciders |
| W.D. Ross, prima facie duties | We have several duties (fidelity, reparation, justice, non-harm) and weigh them | Messy cases with competing duties | Gives no fixed ranking |
| Corrective justice (Aristotle) | Whoever gains from a wrong must repair it | Restitution, reparations | Hard when wrongdoers are long gone |
| Young, social connection model | Responsibility for structural injustice is shared and grows with power, benefit and capacity | Inherited injustice | Less precise about who owes what |
| Capabilities (Sen, Nussbaum) | Justice means people can actually do and be things | Access, development | Lists of capabilities are contested |
| Soft paternalism | Intervene only when a choice isn't informed or voluntary | Minors, addiction, design | Line between soft and hard is blurry |
| Precautionary principle | Under uncertainty with severe possible harm, err toward caution | New technology | Can block useful innovation |

## Principles that run across our cases

Judges sometimes probe consistency. These are the principles we use more than once, so our answers stay coherent.

| Principle | Cases where we use it |
| --- | --- |
| Responsibility grows with power, benefit, reach and capacity | 7, 9, 11, 13, 14 |
| Use the least restrictive tool that works | 7, 10, 12, 13 |
| Whoever decides must not have a stake in the answer | 9, 10, 12, 13 |
| People and their lives are not commodities | 1, 5, 11, 15 |
| Vulnerable people (minors, the uninformed) are owed extra care | 4, 5, 14 |
| Ethics, not law, sets the standard; law can lag behind | All cases |
